# Portfolio Content Audit — Part 2

> **TL;DR:** Your code audit was clean. Your *words* are forgettable.
> Right now your site reads like a competent LinkedIn profile.
> A US/UK founder will land on the hero, read one sentence, and bounce.
> This audit shows you why, and gives you drop-in rewrites for every section.

---

## 1 · Why the content feels boring

After re-reading every section, four things are killing the engagement:

### 1.1 You describe what you do, not what changes for the buyer

The current hero says: *"I help founders worldwide turn attention into revenue — strategy, design and engineering under one roof, custom-coded."*

This is **what you do**. A buyer doesn't care about that — they care about **what changes for them**. Same problem across About, WhyMe, Services, and Pricing.

> *What you do*: "I build custom-coded websites."
> *What changes for them*: "Your competitor's site cost them $40K and 6 months. Yours ships in 3 weeks for $3K."

The first sentence tells me nothing. The second one makes me keep reading.

### 1.2 You lead with the work, not the result

Look at the case studies: they all start with "the brief" — a polished, sanitised story about what the client asked for. The actual **juicy** parts (the "every other agency said 6 months" moment, the "she tried two builders and they fought her audience" tension) are buried halfway down.

> *Current WebSeek case study, first line*: "Most 'AI website builders' produce identical, sectioned-template marketing pages. We built the opposite: custom-coded output, plus a model picker..."
>
> *Better first line*: "Every AI website builder on the market produces the same generic marketing page. We built one that doesn't."

**The "We built the opposite" pattern** — conflict first, then what you did about it — is the only structure that holds attention past the first scroll.

### 1.3 You have no protagonist

In every story on the site, the protagonist is **you**. "I help, I built, I shipped, I run, I design."

But the buyer doesn't want a story about you. They want a story about someone **like them** who had a problem and got it solved. You are the guide, not the hero. The founder you're writing for is the hero.

> Look at your ChinkiZ testimonial: "I did not think it could be achieved without going to e-commerce builders and only using custom code."
>
> The ChinkiZ page opens with **your** build process, not **Chinki's** struggle. Imagine instead:
>
> *"Chinki runs a 600K-subscriber YouTube channel. Her audience watched her knit, then asked in the comments where to buy the needles. She tried Shopify. The theme fought the way her audience shopped. She tried Webflow. Same problem. So we built a custom storefront where the **video sits next to the buy button**."*
>
> Same facts. Completely different emotional pull. The buyer reading this thinks: "That's me. That's my problem. This person fixes my problem."

### 1.4 You're hiding the best lines in the middle of paragraphs

This is the one that hurts the most.

Your **single best proof point** is the school site delivered in 7 days, when other agencies quoted 6 months. It's mentioned in:
- Modern K.B.S. case study (middle of the body)
- WhyMe section (4th reason)
- The testimonial ("Every other agency said 6 months. Delivered in 7 days.")
- The pricing blurb ("Live in 3–4 weeks")
- The stats ("98% retention")

It's the **headline**. It should be in the hero. Right now a foreign prospect has to read 4-5 sections to find it.

Same for:
- *"6 months → 7 days"* (the school story)
- *"10× more premium than our competitors → direct bookings"* (Nandini Travels)
- *"0001–0666, never reprinted"* (CultXberserk)
- *"5 free credits, custom-coded output, not a SaaS"* (WebSeek)

**All four are hero-worthy lines. All four are buried.**

## 2 · The 5 techniques you're missing

Quick framework, then rewrites.

### 2.1 Open with a result, not a service

| ❌ Service-first | ✅ Result-first |
|---|---|
| "I'm a web designer and developer." | "I built a school site in 7 days. Other agencies had quoted 6 months." |
| "I help founders worldwide turn attention into revenue." | "Your competitor's site cost $40K and 6 months. Mine starts at $3K and ships in 3 weeks." |

**Why it works:** the buyer's first question is *"what will this do for me?"* — a service description can't answer that. A specific result can.

### 2.2 Use specific numbers, not vague ones

| ❌ Vague | ✅ Specific |
|---|---|
| "20+ projects" | "20+ products live, 9 with full case studies" |
| "Built a school site" | "Delivered Modern K.B.S. in 7 days. Other agencies had quoted 6 months." |
| "Founder-friendly" | "I run my own product (WebSeek.ai), so I think in your terms — speed, ownership, ROI" |
| "Conversion-focused" | "Every project starts from how your buyers decide" |
| "Custom-coded" | "Custom React, Next.js, Astro — no Shopify, no Webflow, no monthly platform tax" |

**Why it works:** vague copy is the same as every other portfolio. Specific copy is unforgettable.

### 2.3 Put conflict before solution

The classic story structure. Every great case study has it.

| ❌ Solution-first | ✅ Conflict-first |
|---|---|
| "WebSeek.ai is a custom-coded AI website builder with a model picker for Claude, GPT and Gemini." | "Every AI website builder on the market produces the same generic marketing page. So we built the opposite." |
| "A high-fidelity prototype before a line of code is written." | "Most designers show you a static mockup. You sign off. Then the dev builds it. Then the dev shows you something that doesn't match the mockup. We skip all of that with a clickable prototype." |

**Why it works:** a problem they recognise is a hook. A solution they didn't ask for is a pitch.

### 2.4 Make the buyer the hero, not you

> *"I help founders worldwide…"* — you are the hero.
>
> *"Your competitor just launched a site that cost them $40K. Yours can be live in 3 weeks for $3K."* — buyer is the hero.

When the buyer is the hero, the copy feels like it's *for them*. When you are the hero, it feels like it's about you.

### 2.5 Cut every adjective that doesn't earn its place

Adjectives are the enemy of memorable copy. Audit yours:

> *"I help founders worldwide turn **attention into revenue** — **strategy**, design and **engineering** under one roof, **custom-coded**."*

Three of those four phrases are filler. The one that earns its place is "custom-coded" (because it differentiates from page-builder templates).

> *Leaner version: "Custom-coded websites that ship in 3 weeks. No page-builder markup. You own every line."*

Every word does work.

## 6 · Storytelling patterns to apply to case studies

Every case study should follow this 5-beat structure:

### Beat 1 — The hook
> "Every AI website builder on the market produces the same generic marketing page."
> "Kangra's most-reviewed taxi operator was losing every lead to Google Maps."
> "A 600K-subscriber YouTuber tried two e-commerce builders. Both fought her audience."

### Beat 2 — The stakes
> "She was 6 weeks from the next creator drop. The Shopify theme wasn't going to ship in time."

### Beat 3 — What we did
> "We built a custom Next.js storefront with a video gallery that mirrored her YouTube grid."

### Beat 4 — The result (specific)
> "Live in 14 days. Razorpay integrated. Video next to the buy button. Now she ships product drops every Tuesday."

### Beat 5 — The lesson / takeaway (optional but powerful)
> "Most e-commerce builders are built for catalog businesses, not creator brands. If your buyers shop on *videos*, you need a store that puts the video first."

**Most of your case studies currently jump from Beat 1 (the brief) to Beat 3 (what we shipped), skipping the tension (Beat 2) and the lesson (Beat 5).** That's where the storytelling is missing.

## 7 · Voice rules (apply everywhere)

After the rewrites, lock these in for any new copy you write:

1. **Result before service.** Lead with the change for the buyer, not what you do.
2. **Specific before vague.** Use exact numbers, names, dates. Cut every "various", "multiple", "many".
3. **Conflict before solution.** Name the problem first. Then what you did.
4. **Buyer is the hero.** Use "you" and "your" more than "I" and "my".
5. **Cut every adjective that doesn't earn its place.** If you can delete it without losing meaning, delete it.
6. **End on the lesson, not the features.** "This is why I built it" is more memorable than "This is what's in it".

## 9 · One more thing — voice

Your current copy sounds like **a developer writing for other developers**. That's fine for technical buyers, but most of your cold-email targets are founders, not engineers. They want a story, not a feature list.

Read your hero aloud. Does it sound like something you'd say at a dinner party? If not, rewrite it until it does.

The best portfolio copy in 2026 sounds like:
- A founder telling a peer what they did
- A case study at a YC dinner
- A tweet thread from someone who shipped something impressive

It doesn't sound like a services page. It sounds like **a story you want to tell at a bar**.

That's the bar.
